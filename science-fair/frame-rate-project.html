Frame Rate & Persistence of Vision1

Background research
How do your eyes and brain see motion?

A movie is just a lot of still pictures shown very quickly, one after another. Each still picture is called a frame. How many frames are shown each second is the frame rate, measured in frames per second (FPS). Somewhere along the way your brain stops seeing a slideshow and starts seeing motion. That's the moment this project is trying to find.

The trip from picture to brain

  1. Cornea and lens bend light to focus the picture.
  2. The retina at the back of the eye has about 120 million rods (for dim light and movement) and 6 million cones (for color and detail). They turn light into electrical signals.
  3. The optic nerve carries those signals to the back of the brain. Area V1 sees shapes and edges, and an area called MT works out what is moving and which way.

Persistence of vision

After a picture disappears, your eye and brain keep a fading trace of it for a split second, roughly 1/15 to 1/10 of a second. If the next picture shows up before the trace fades, you don't notice the gap. Try it: stare at a bright window, then close your eyes and you'll see an afterimage.

The phi phenomenon

When two pictures flash one after the other in slightly different places, your brain fills in the movement between them, even though nothing actually moved. Scientists call this apparent motion. It's the same reason a row of blinking lights looks like it's "running."

Scientist's note

For about 100 years, people said "persistence of vision" was the whole reason movies work. Today scientists think the brain's motion detectors (the phi phenomenon) do most of the work. Persistence of vision mostly explains why you don't see the dark gaps between frames. Explaining the difference will impress the judges.

Plan the experiment
Question, hypothesis and variables

Testable question

At what frame rate (FPS) do most people stop seeing separate drawings and start seeing smooth motion?

My hypothesis

Use the form "If… then… because…". Write your own guess before testing anyone. A wrong guess is still good science!

Example: "If I show my car chase at different speeds, then most people will see smooth motion at about 12 FPS, because cartoons are often drawn at 12 drawings per second."

Rating scale (read this to every volunteer)

ScoreWhat it looks like

Key definition: the "movie threshold"

For each volunteer, the movie threshold is the lowest FPS where they answered "movie" and rated it 4 or 5. This is the number you'll average at the end.

Get ready
Materials list

There are two ways to show the animation. Use both if you can. The digital flipbook gives exact, fair FPS numbers for the experiment. The zoetrope is the eye-catching machine for the display table, and it shows how people watched "movies" in the 1800s.

For the drawings (everyone)

  • Plain white paper or index cards
  • Pencil, eraser and a black fine-tip marker
  • Ruler
  • Light source for tracing (a window or tablet screen)
  • Colored pencils (optional)

Option A: Digital flipbook

  • Phone or tablet to photograph the drawings
  • A computer, tablet or phone to play them back
  • An app that lets you set the FPS exactly, such as Stop Motion Studio (free) or Scratch (free, scratch.mit.edu)
  • Or the built-in FPS tester in the online version of this guide

Option B: Zoetrope

  • Black card stock (2–3 sheets) or a sturdy cardboard cake round
  • A round base about 8–10 inches across (a cake pan, a pizza box circle or a paper plate stack)
  • Scissors or a craft knife (adult use only)
  • Tape, glue stick and a ruler
  • A small piece of bright tape to mark one spot

Something to spin it (pick one):

  • Record turntable: the easiest choice, with steady speeds of 33⅓, 45 and sometimes 78 RPM
  • Lego motor (Technic, Powered Up or Spike) with gears to slow it down
  • Lazy Susan spun by hand (fun, but the speed isn't steady)
  • Bike wheel with the bike flipped upside down (adult supervision only, keep fingers away from the spokes)

For collecting data

  • A stopwatch or phone timer
  • Printed data sheets (page 9) and a clipboard
  • At least 10 volunteers. More is better: 15–20 makes the results more trustworthy. Try for a mix of ages: kids, teens, parents and grandparents.
  • Index cards with the rating scale printed on them, so volunteers can point to a number
Safety
  • An adult does all craft-knife cutting.
  • Never touch a spinning wheel or turntable. Stop it first.
  • Ask volunteers if flashing or flickering images bother them. Anyone with a history of seizures or migraines should skip the test.

Engineering build
Build a zoetrope

A zoetrope ("wheel of life") is a spinning drum with slits. You look through the slits at drawings on the inside wall. Each slit works like a camera shutter: you see one drawing for a split second, then darkness, then the next drawing. That flash-and-gap pattern is why the pictures don't blur together.

Build steps

  1. Pick the number of frames. Use 12. It divides evenly and is enough for a smooth loop.
  2. Measure the drum. Circumference = 3.14 × diameter. For an 8-inch base, that's about 25 inches. Each drawing slot is then 25 ÷ 12 ≈ 2.1 inches wide.
  3. Make the wall. Tape black card strips together into a band about 4 inches tall and as long as the circumference, plus 0.5 inch for overlap.
  4. Cut 12 slits along the top inch of the band, evenly spaced (2.1 inches apart). Each slit is about ⅛ inch wide and 1 inch tall. Narrower slits give a sharper image but a darker one.
  5. Glue the band around the base, with the slits at the top.
  6. Put the picture strip (page 7) inside, along the bottom of the wall, with each drawing lined up between two slits.
  7. Stick a piece of bright tape on the outside of the drum. You'll use it to count spins.

Zoetrope math: turning spins into FPS

FPS = drawings on the strip × spins per second

To find spins per second: count how many times the tape mark passes you in 10 seconds, then divide by 10. Do it 3 times and take the average.

Engineering build
Make a digital flipbook

The digital version is the main measuring tool for the experiment, because a computer can show each drawing for an exact amount of time. The zoetrope's speed wobbles a little.

Step 1: Photograph the drawings

  • Tape the phone to a stack of books, pointing straight down, so every photo is taken from the same spot.
  • Tape a paper "corner guide" on the table so each drawing sits in exactly the same place.
  • Use the same lighting for every photo, with no shadows from your hand.
  • Name the photos in order: frame01, frame02, … frame12.

Step 2: Play them back at an exact FPS (pick one tool)

Stop Motion Studio

A free app for phone or tablet. Import the photos, then open the movie settings and set the frame rate. Export one video per speed, for example car_4fps.mp4.

Scratch (coding!)

Upload each drawing as a costume on one sprite. Then build:

Step 3: Choose your test speeds

Most screens refresh 60 times per second. Frame rates that divide evenly into 60 give each drawing the same amount of screen time. Those are the fairest speeds to test:

FPS2461012152030
Each drawing is shown for0.50 s0.25 s0.17 s0.10 s0.083 s0.067 s0.05 s0.033 s
Screen refreshes per drawing30151065432

8 and 24 FPS don't divide evenly into 60, so some drawings stay on screen a little longer than others. That causes a tiny stutter, called judder. You can still test them. Movies at 24 FPS have the same problem on TVs, and the fix is called "3:2 pulldown." That makes a great fun fact for the board.

Make it a fair test: don't go slow-to-fast every time

If speeds always go from slow to fast, volunteers can guess the pattern and say "smooth" too early. Use one of these:

  • Mystery order: shuffle the speeds for each volunteer. You can write each speed on a card and draw cards from a hat.
  • Up and down: go slow to fast once, then fast to slow once, and average the two thresholds.

The art part
Draw the car chase

The movie is a 12-frame loop: after frame 12 it goes straight back to frame 1 and keeps repeating. A loop is perfect for both the zoetrope and the flipbook.

Story idea: "The Getaway"

A red race car speeds along a road, with a police car close behind. The road lines and a tree slide backward to show speed. The wheels spin, and the race car bounces a little over bumps.

Animator tricks
  • Trace the last drawing on a window or bright screen, then change just a little.
  • Small steps: move things only about ¼ car-length per frame. Big jumps look choppy even at high FPS. That's an extension experiment on page 11!
  • Draw the road line in exactly the same place on every frame so it doesn't wobble.
  • Use thick, dark outlines. They show up best through zoetrope slits.
Check your loop

Stack the 12 drawings and flip through them with your thumb like a flipbook. If something jumps between frame 12 and frame 1, redraw frame 12 so it leads smoothly back into frame 1.

The dashed line is the road, so keep it in the same place in every box. For an 8-inch drum, print this page at 100% and each box will be about 2 inches wide, which fits well.

Run it
Experiment procedure

Set up (same for every volunteer)

  1. Use the same room, with the lights at the same brightness each time.
  2. Seat the volunteer about 2 feet from the screen. Put a tape mark on the floor for the chair.
  3. If they wear glasses for distance or reading screens, have them wear them, and write it on the data sheet.
  4. Prepare a shuffled list of speeds for this volunteer: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 24, 30 FPS.

The script (read the same words every time)

"I'm going to show you a short cartoon at different speeds. Each one plays for 5 seconds. After each one, tell me: did it look like separate pictures or like a movie? Then give it a smoothness score from 1 to 5 using this card. There are no right or wrong answers."

For each speed

  1. Play the animation for 5 seconds. Use a timer.
  2. Ask: "Pictures or movie?" Write P or M.
  3. Ask: "Smoothness score, 1 to 5?" Write the number.
  4. Give them a 3-second break by looking away, then do the next speed.

After all speeds

  1. Find this volunteer's movie threshold: the lowest FPS where they said M and gave a score of 4 or 5.
  2. Optional zoetrope round: let them watch the zoetrope at 2 or 3 turntable speeds and score it the same way. Does the zoetrope look smoother than the screen at the same FPS? (The dark gap from each slit can help!)
  3. Thank them!

Write down for each volunteer

  • A code like V1, V2 or V3 instead of their real name
  • Age group: under 10, 10–17, 18–49, 50+
  • Glasses or contacts: yes or no
  • Plays video games often: yes or no (fun extra!)
Good-scientist habits
  • Don't hint, smile or say "good" when they answer.
  • Don't let volunteers watch each other's turns.
  • Test yourself last, because you know the animation too well. Mark your result as "the scientist."
  • Write down anything surprising in a notes column.

Make sense of it
Graphs and analysis

Graph 1: Average smoothness score at each frame rate (line graph)

Plot the "Average score" row from the data sheet, then join the dots. Where does the line cross the dashed "smooth" line? That's the group's movie threshold.

Graph 2: How many people had each movie threshold (bar graph)

Along the bottom, list each FPS. Draw a bar showing how many volunteers had that FPS as their threshold. Is there one tall bar (people agree), or are the bars spread out (people's eyes and brains differ)?

Do the math

Average (mean) threshold = add up all the thresholds ÷ the number of volunteers

= ÷ = FPS

Range = highest threshold − lowest threshold

= − = FPS

Questions to think about

  • Was there a speed where almost everyone switched from "pictures" to "movie"?
  • Did scores keep going up after 24 FPS, or did they level off? (A curve that flattens out is called a plateau.)
  • Did kids and adults differ? People with and without glasses? Gamers and non-gamers?
  • Did the zoetrope look smoother or choppier than the screen at about the same FPS? Why might that be?

Sources of error to mention

  • Volunteers might guess what you want to hear, which is why you shuffle the speeds and use a script.
  • The zoetrope's speed changes slightly as it spins.
  • Screens can't show some frame rates perfectly evenly (judder at 8 and 24 FPS).
  • A small number of volunteers: more people give more trustworthy averages.

Extension experiments (pick one if there's time)

Big steps vs. small steps

Make a second animation where the car moves twice as far each frame. Does it need a higher FPS to look smooth? (Prediction: yes. Smoothness depends on both FPS and how far things jump.)

Center vs. side vision

Have volunteers look at a dot to the side of the screen. The edges of the retina have more rods, which are very sensitive to flicker and motion. Does the animation look choppier out of the corner of the eye?

Bright vs. dim

Repeat the test with the screen brightness turned way down. Eyes respond more slowly in dim light. Does the threshold change?

Flicker fusion (micro:bit or Arduino)

Blink an LED faster and faster. At what speed does it stop flickering and look steady? For most people it's around 50–60 flashes per second, which is much faster than the movie threshold. Why are they different?

Eye-doctor connection

Eye doctors and vision scientists use flicker and motion tests to check how well the retina, optic nerve and brain are working together. Some eye conditions make it harder to see flicker or motion. That makes this project a real (simple) version of a vision test.

What results to expect (keep this to yourself until after testing, so it doesn't bias the hypothesis)

  • Most people start to see some motion around 6–10 FPS, and rate hand-drawn animation as smooth somewhere around 12–20 FPS.
  • Scores usually plateau above about 20–24 FPS. Hand-drawn animation already looks "finished" there, which is part of why 24 FPS became the movie standard.
  • The threshold shifts a lot with how far objects move per frame. Small steps look smooth at lower FPS. If results are surprisingly high, the drawings may have big jumps, and that is itself a great finding to discuss.
  • Differences between kids and adults, or gamers and non-gamers, are usually small with only 10–20 people. "No clear difference" is a perfectly valid result.

Where you can help

  • All craft-knife cutting and any work near a spinning wheel or motor.
  • Setting up the app or Scratch project the first time, then let him run it.
  • Recruiting volunteers (neighbors, family, classmates at pickup).
  • Checking the math on averages, but let him do it first.
Check the fair's rules

Many school and regional science fairs treat any project with human volunteers as a "human subjects" project. That can require a simple consent form or teacher approval before testing starts. Ask the teacher early. Use codes (V1, V2…) instead of names on the board, and skip anyone who is sensitive to flashing images.
